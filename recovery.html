Cardio + recovery

Cardio

For a mass-and-strength goal, cardio's cost is real but narrow. Across 43 studies, concurrent training did not reduce hypertrophy (SMD −0.01) or maximal strength (−0.06), but it did reduce explosive strength (−0.28), more so when both were done in the same session (Schumann 2022). The exception that applies to this lifter: trained individuals showed a lower-body maximal-strength penalty that untrained people did not, and it was larger when both were done in the same session (Petré 2021). Interference grows with endurance frequency and duration (Wilson 2012). Running showed a type I fiber-growth penalty that cycling did not (Lundberg 2022), but the evidence on running versus cycling conflicts (Sabag 2018). Lifting before cardio protected lower-body 1RM (+3.96 kg) (Murlasits 2017). Separating the two by 6–24 h beat doing them back to back (Robineau 2016).

The case for keeping cardio is strong. Each additional MET of fitness is associated with 11–17% lower all-cause mortality (BJSM umbrella review 2024; Kodama 2009). These are observational associations. Endurance performance can be maintained on as little as two sessions a week if intensity is preserved (Spiering 2021), although much of that evidence comes from non-athletes.

Sleep + recovery

Sleep directly affects how much of the training turns into muscle. One night without sleep cut postprandial muscle protein synthesis by 18%, raised cortisol 21% and lowered testosterone 24% (Lamon 2021). In dieters, 5.5 h instead of 8.5 h of sleep opportunity raised the share of weight lost as lean mass by about 60% (Nedeltcheva 2010). That study was small and untrained, but the direction is clear. Several consecutive short nights reduce force in multi-joint lifts specifically (Knowles 2018), and acute sleep loss of 6 h or less produces small but real strength losses, worst with late bedtimes (Craven 2022 via SBS). Expert consensus says athletes tend to sleep less than 7 h, and that a single 7–9 h target should give way to individual need (Walsh 2021). For a 6-day lifter who also runs, the working target is at least 7 h asleep, ideally 8–9 h in bed, on a consistent schedule, with extra care before Monday and Wednesday (heavy squat and deadlift) and during test week I.

For readiness, RPE/RIR autoregulation matches velocity-based training for strength in trained lifters and costs nothing (Hickmott 2022). HRV-guided training has weak, mixed evidence and is best used as a trend signal, not a daily go/no-go (2024 HRV review). Injury risk in powerlifting is low (1.0–4.4 injuries per 1,000 h), but training-limiting pain is common, mostly in the low back, shoulder and elbow (Berglund 2024). That is the main argument for cutting four weekly hinges to two and taking out the extra pressing volume. The acute:chronic workload ratio is a poor injury predictor (Impellizzeri 2021). Avoiding abrupt jumps in load and volume is still sensible practice, which is why TM increases are capped I.
