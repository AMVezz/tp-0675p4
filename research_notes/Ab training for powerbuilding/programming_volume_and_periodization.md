## 1. Weekly direct-set volume and frequency for abs (MEV/MAV/MRV)

### Takeaway
Coach guidance (Renaissance Periodization) puts ab MEV near zero, since heavy squats and deadlifts already train the trunk, with productive volume (MAV) around 16–20 direct sets/week and MRV around 25 sets/week, spread over 3–5 (up to 4–6) sessions. Peer-reviewed dose-response work shows that more weekly sets bring more growth, with diminishing returns per session. The lifter's current 6 sets/week sits at the low end, so tripling it is well within the range coaches consider recoverable.

### Cited Findings
- **[Coach consensus]** RP's ab guidance holds that abs can grow with zero direct sets/week (MV/MEV ≈ 0) because they work hard during compound lifts. The RP help-centre article suggests a starting MEV of about 2 sets/week for beginners and about 6 sets/week for experienced trainees. — [RP Help Center: Abs](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/32433381453463-Abs) (search excerpt; page not fetchable)
- **[Coach consensus]** RP's older ab article says serious recovery problems tend to appear above about 25 sets/week, "though some people can train far in excess of that amount," and recommends a frequency of 3–5 sessions/week. — [RP: Ab Hypertrophy Training Tips](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/ab-training); [repost on Scribd](https://es.scribd.com/document/371274580)
- **[Third-party/secondary]** A spreadsheet built on RP's values lists abs at MAV 16–20 sets/week, MRV 25, frequency 3–5x/week. — [Google Sheets RP landmarks](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1Dc0oZnkxcFVZ6Cl8whOs2O1AiXNKSkhRCRJmJ103_QA/edit)
- **[Conflicting secondary]** A different landmark table lists abs at MAV 4–12 and MRV 12–20. It does not make clear which muscle group the row covers, so treat it as unconfirmed. This disagrees with the 16–20/25 figures above. — [arvo.guru RP Training summary](https://arvo.guru/es/resources/methods/rp-training); [skills.sh rp-training](https://skills.sh/borisghidaglia/science-based-lifter/rp-training)
- **[Coach consensus]** The RP help article suggests starting at 2x/week and working up toward MRV, with progression to 4–6x/week for people pushing ab training hard. — [RP Help Center: Abs](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/32433381453463-Abs) (search excerpt)
- **[Peer-reviewed, preprint→published]** Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos et al. ran dose-response meta-regressions (67 studies in the preprint). The posterior probability that the volume slope exceeds zero was 100% for both hypertrophy and strength. Frequency had consistently identifiable effects for strength but not hypertrophy, once volume is held equal. — [SportRxiv preprint v2](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460); [bibbase record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/joshuapelland-jacobremmert-zacrobinson-sethhinson-michaelzourdos-theresistancetrainingdoseresponsemetaregressionsexploringtheeectsofweeklyvolumeandfrequencyonmusclehypertrophyandstrengthgain-2024)
- **[Peer-reviewed, preprint]** A follow-up per-session analysis (Remmert et al.) found diminishing returns as sets per session increased. The inflection came at about 11 fractional sets per session for hypertrophy and about 2 direct sets per session for strength. — [SportRxiv preprint 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** The fractional set-counting method (indirect sets counted as 0.5) fit best in Pelland et al. This means squats and deadlifts plausibly give the trunk some indirect "credit." — [SportRxiv preprint v2](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** The Martuscello et al. systematic review of core EMG concluded that free-weight squats and deadlifts are favored over traditional core exercises for some core muscles, especially lumbar multifidus (moderate evidence). However, the 2020 Oliva-Lozano & Muyor update found higher internal-oblique activity in core-stability exercises and higher multifidus activity in traditional exercises. — [Martuscello review record](https://latech.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/systematic-review-of-core-muscle-activity-during-physical-fitness/); [Oliva-Lozano & Muyor 2020, IJERPH](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/12/4306)
- **[Peer-reviewed, weak]** I found no human data on rectus abdominis recovery time. A 1979 biopsy study found large between-person variation in abdominal fiber-type composition but only minor differences between the abdominal muscles. The common claim that abs are "mixed fiber, recover fast" comes from popular sources. — [Fibre types in human abdominal muscles (PubMed 161688)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/161688/); [Breaking Muscle (secondary)](https://breakingmuscle.com/eccentric-abdominal-training/)

### Inferences
- For this lifter, roughly 12–20 direct ab sets/week across 4–6 sessions is a defensible "tons of abs" target. That works out to about 3–4 sets per session, which keeps each session below the per-session diminishing-returns point. Starting near 10–12 sets and adding about 2 sets/week through accumulation follows RP-style progression.
- Because frequency adds little to hypertrophy once volume is equal, the main reason to train abs 4–6x/week is logistical: short 6–10 minute blocks fit sessions that already run 45–75 minutes. It is not physiologically required.
- The trunk already gets substantial indirect work from six barbell-heavy days. Counting that work fractionally argues against pushing direct volume to the top of the RP range.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study establishes ab-specific MEV/MAV/MRV. These are coach heuristics, and the RP figures conflict across secondary sources.
- I found no human data on ab recovery kinetics after hard sets (CK, soreness, strength recovery time course), so "abs recover fast and can be trained daily" is coach lore, not evidence.

## 2. Is there a point where more ab volume stops helping or harms recovery for heavy lifts?

### Takeaway
Yes, in principle. Per-session and weekly volume both show diminishing returns, and RP's coach estimate is that recovery problems appear around 25+ sets/week. I found no study that directly measures ab-volume interference with squat or deadlift performance. The bigger practical risks are acute trunk fatigue at the wrong time (see section 3) and session-length creep.

### Cited Findings
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Weekly volume relates positively to growth with diminishing returns. A 2026 reanalysis that cites the Pelland work (220 hypertrophy effects, 35 studies) found frequency at a given weekly volume "compatible with negligible effects." — [Preprints.org 202610.1022](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202610.1022)
- **[Peer-reviewed, preprint]** Hypertrophy returns per session flatten at about 11 fractional sets, and strength returns at about 2 direct sets. — [SportRxiv preprint 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- **[Coach consensus]** RP: serious recovery problems tend to appear above about 25 ab sets/week. — [RP: Ab Hypertrophy Training Tips](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/ab-training)
- **[Coach consensus/clinical]** McGill's work, as summarized by ACE, argues that trunk endurance, not maximal strength, helps people avoid awkward spinal postures. The same summary notes that the chance of lifting with a deviated back position rises with fatigue. McGill-based protocols use reverse-pyramid sets "to build muscular endurance without fatiguing the muscle." — [ACE: McGill's Big Three](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/7077/low-back-exercises-stuart-mcgill-s-big-three/?topicScope=article); [McGill 2010, Core Training, SCJ (PDF)](https://www.backfitpro.com/medical-scientific-articles/2010/[70]McGill,S.M.(2010)Core-training[J.Strength-and-Condit.].pdf)

### Inferences
- The ceiling that matters for a powerbuilder is the timing of acute trunk fatigue and total systemic and time cost, not the absolute number of ab sets. Twenty sets a week placed well is probably safer than 10 sets placed right before heavy deadlifts.
- Use performance markers as autoregulation. If squat or deadlift bracing feels compromised, or the low back is sore going into Monday or Wednesday, cut ab volume (especially loaded flexion and long-lever anti-extension) before cutting main-lift work.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled trial comparing different direct-ab volumes against powerlifting 1RM outcomes or recovery markers in trained lifters.

## 3. Does training abs before heavy squats/deadlifts impair performance or spinal stability? Best placement

### Takeaway
I found no study that isolates abdominal pre-fatigue before barbell squats or deadlifts. Related peer-reviewed work shows that trunk and lumbar fatigue reduces dynamic spinal stability, alters lumbar-pelvic coordination, raises estimated spinal loading and changes squat kinematics. Together with coach consensus, this supports doing fatiguing ab work after the main lifts (end of session), and keeping only light, low-volume activation (e.g., McGill Big 3) in the warm-up.

### Cited Findings
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Lumbar extensor fatigue changed lumbar-pelvic rotation rhythm and associated L5/S1 loading during lifting and lowering (Hu & Ning; 13–15 subjects). The authors suggest altered coordination after fatigue may increase spinal loading and injury risk. — [CDC Stacks (Hu & Ning)](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/203817/cdc_203817_DS1.pdf); [CDC Stacks related study](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/203820/cdc_203820_DS1.pdf)
- **[Peer-reviewed/dissertation]** Torso dynamic stability (maximum Lyapunov exponent) decreased with trunk muscle fatigue during repetitive movement. — [Gottipati, Virginia Tech dissertation](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstreams/0c12efa6-e605-4bfc-a2a1-5b9d006edc5e/download)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Repetitive lifting fatigued trunk muscles (EMG) and changed trunk flexion angle and angular velocity in 18 men. — [Kazemi et al. 2023, J Health Saf Work](https://journals.tums.ac.ir/jhsw/article-1-6716-en.pdf)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Core muscle fatigue significantly decreased knee flexion and increased knee valgus during double-leg squats in 16 female bodybuilders, with no hip-flexion change (one-group pre/post). — [Seyedi et al. 2022, Physical Treatments](https://doaj.org/article/58d670ffd44048ab8b074bd06b54415d)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Nomura et al. compared repeated back squats with and without an erector-spinae fatigue task and measured forward trunk inclination. The direction of the effect is unclear from the available abstract excerpts. — [ISBS 2015 proceedings](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/6386)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** A highly fatiguing barbell session (bench, deadlift, squat) altered back-squat mechanics afterward, with less knee and hip flexion despite instructions to keep form. — [Effects of fatigue from resistance training on barbell back squat biomechanics (PubMed 24662156)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24662156/)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Core stability is described as a key determinant of squat and deadlift performance above about 80% 1RM. — [JSSM 11(1):26](https://jssm.org/11-1-26.p_d_f)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** A preemptive voluntary abdominal contraction (bracing) increased performance time in squat variations (p = .0011). This supports bracing practice and activation, not fatigue. — [IJSPT 2023 via PMC (search excerpt)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5357260)
- **[Coach consensus]** McGill Big 3 (curl-up, side plank, bird dog) is widely recommended as a pre-lift warm-up to prepare the trunk for squats and deadlifts, in low-fatigue reverse-pyramid holds. — [ACE: McGill's Big Three](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/7077/low-back-exercises-stuart-mcgill-s-big-three/?topicScope=article); [Tom's Guide (secondary)](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/workouts/stop-doing-sit-ups-a-kettlebell-instructor-explains-why-the-mcgill-big-3-protects-your-spine-better-while-strengthening-your-core)

### Inferences
- **Placement:** put fatiguing ab work (loaded crunches, leg raises, ab wheel, long-lever planks to near failure) at the end of each session, after all barbell work. Do it on heavy squat and deadlift days only after the main lifts are finished. In the warm-up, use only brief bracing and Big-3 style activation.
- **Day-before rule (inference, not tested):** the heavy days are Mon (squat) and Wed (deadlift), and Fri (paused squat) and Sat (RDL) are also spine-loading. The days before them are Sun (rest), Tue (Push A) and Thu (Push B). Keep the hardest loaded-flexion and high-volume ab work off Sunday and away from Tuesday evening. Push days are reasonable for moderate anti-movement work. Thursday precedes the Friday paused squat, so keep it moderate.
- A practical weekly distribution: heavier loaded flexion on Mon (after squats) and Fri (after paused squats). Anti-extension and anti-rotation on Tue and Thu (push days, little spinal loading). A light oblique/carry finisher on Sat after RDLs. Wednesday is optional and light, after deadlifts, or skipped.
- The squat kinematic changes after core fatigue (knee valgus, altered flexion) argue specifically against "ab circuits as warm-ups" before heavy lower-body work.

### Gaps
- No study found tests abdominal (flexor) pre-fatigue before barbell squat or deadlift 1RM or RPE performance in trained lifters. Most evidence concerns extensor fatigue, bodyweight or single-leg squats, or occupational lifting with small samples.
- No evidence was found on next-day effects (ab training the day before heavy squats or deadlifts). The day-before guidance is inference.

## 4. How established powerbuilding/powerlifting programs handle abs

### Takeaway
Mainstream strength programs treat abs as low-priority accessory work at the end of sessions. 5/3/1 uses a "single-leg/core" slot of about 50–100 total reps per session. PHAT and PHUL-style programs list optional 2–3 sets of weighted crunches or woodchoppers. Nippard recommends combining weighted flexion with anti-extension and anti-rotation work. No major program found prescribes "tons" of ab work, and all place it after the main lifts.

### Cited Findings
- **[Coach consensus]** In 5/3/1 (Wendler), modern variants assign one push, one pull and one single-leg/core assistance exercise per day, at 50–100 total reps per category. Set count is unimportant, and examples include hanging leg raises and ab-wheel rollouts. — [Lift Vault: Main/Supplemental/Assistance](https://liftvault.com/resources/main-supplemental-assistance-work/); [5/3/1 Primer (fitness wiki)](https://thefitness.wiki/5-3-1-primer/) (search excerpt)
- **[Coach consensus]** Wendler's Boring But Big template lists "Abs – 5 sets" as an assistance item. — [JimWendler.com: Boring But Big](https://jimwendler.com/blogs/jimwendler-com/101077382-boring-but-big)
- **[Coach anecdote]** An elitefts log describes adding an ab progression that starts at 50 total reps on top of existing ab work within 5/3/1. — [elitefts: The Fifties](https://elitefts.com/blogs/training/the-fifties)
- **[Third-party/secondary]** PHAT (Layne Norton) third-party versions list weighted crunches at 2×6–10 on a lower-body day and woodchoppers at 3×8–12 on a back/shoulder day. Assistance work generally runs 2–3×6–10. No official Norton ab prescription was found. — [FitnessVolt PHAT](https://fitnessvolt.com/layne-nortons-phat-workout-for-size-and-strength/); [Liftosaur PHAT](https://www.liftosaur.com/programs/phat)
- **[Coach consensus]** Nippard argues that the best total core training combines weighted spinal flexion (crunches), anti-rotation (half-kneeling Pallof) and anti-extension (planks), with progressive overload. Once 3×20–40 s long-lever planks are easy, move to harder variations. His Pure Bodybuilding PPL places core work on push days at 2–4 sets per exercise. — [FitnessVolt: Nippard 6-pack tips](https://fitnessvolt.com/jeff-nippard-6-pack-abs-tips/); [BarBend: Pure Bodybuilding review](https://barbend.com/news/jeff-nippard-pure-bodybuilding-program/)

### Inferences
- The lifter's current 6 sets/week is roughly equal to the prescriptions of established strength programs. "Tons of abs" (12–20 sets/week) exceeds typical powerbuilding templates but stays within RP hypertrophy ranges.
- Borrowing 5/3/1's rep-total approach (e.g., 50–100 reps of one ab movement per session, any set structure) is a simple, time-efficient template that suits supersetting.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the exact ab prescriptions in Nippard's Powerbuilding System, Juggernaut Method, Sheiko or Greg Nuckols/SBS programs, because product pages and Boostcamp were not fetchable and search excerpts lacked those details.

## 5. Adjusting ab volume across deload, peak and 1RM test weeks

### Takeaway
Powerlifting taper evidence (peer-reviewed but limited) supports cutting volume about 40–70% over the final 1–2 weeks while keeping intensity high, with the last hard session about 3–4 days before testing. Accessory work, including abs, is the natural first place to cut. Ab work should follow or exceed the main-lift volume reductions in weeks 6, 11 and 12.

### Cited Findings
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Elite NZ raw powerlifters reduced training volume about 59% during the taper while holding intensity steady or slightly lower. The last session came about 3.7 days before competition, and tapering was mostly guided by trial and error and "feel." — [Pritchard et al. 2016, JSCR (PubMed 26670988)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26670988/); [author accepted version](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/2455/3/Pritchard%20et%20al%20author%20accepted%20version%5B1%5D.pdf)
- **[Peer-reviewed/thesis]** Pritchard's AUT doctoral work, which included a one-week taper with about 70% volume reduction, concluded that athletes should make substantial volume reductions with little change to intensity. — [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/10979)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Travis, Mujika, Gentles, Stone & Bazyler (2020, *Sports* 8(9):125) reviewed tapering and peaking for powerlifting. They found very limited evidence on tapering for maximal strength, and noted that many studies lack detail practitioners could use. — [Travis et al. 2020 (ETSU record)](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works-2/370); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/886093729f784abda2365f6ae95125f9)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** A systematic review from the same research area suggests the optimal taper for maximal strength may be ≤2 weeks with volume-load halved while intensity is kept or reduced. Strength tapering appears more sensitive to volume-load reductions and taper duration than to intensity changes. — [PMC8582352 (search excerpt)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8582352); [Frontiers in Physiology 2021, 10.3389/fphys.2021.735932](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.735932/pdf). Note: the excerpts did not clearly attribute which paper made the "≤2 weeks / half volume-load" claim, so verify.
- **[Peer-reviewed]** A survey of 364 North American powerlifters found the most common practice was a 7–10 day step taper with a 41–50% reduction in volume-load. A 2021 trial in 16 powerlifters found step and exponential tapers produced similar results, with deadlift 1RM changes favoring the exponential taper. — [Travis et al. search summary, PMC7552788 / Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.735932/pdf)
- **[Coach consensus]** Stronger by Science's tapering article reports that a 2015 review found keeping or slightly increasing intensity had greater effects on strength than reducing it. — [Stronger by Science: Tapering](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/tapering/) (search excerpt)

### Inferences
- **Week 6 (deload):** halve ab sets (e.g., 16 → 8) and drop to 2–3 sessions. Keep exercises familiar and stop well short of failure, mirroring the program's "half volume, lighter" rule.
- **Weeks 7–10 (intensification):** hold ab volume at or slightly below the end of accumulation (e.g., 12–16 sets). Main-lift intensity rises and bracing demand under 85–92.5% loads is high, so avoid adding new, sore-making ab exercises.
- **Week 11 (peak, ~half volume):** cut ab work about 50–70% (e.g., 4–8 sets total). Keep it to low-fatigue bracing and anti-movement work, with no loaded flexion to failure.
- **Week 12 (test week):** minimal or no direct ab work 72–96 hours before the squat (Mon) and deadlift (Thu) tests, consistent with Pritchard's last-session timing of about 3.7 days. Brief Big-3 activation in warm-ups is fine. Light ab work can resume after Thursday's deadlift, if at all, before Friday's OHP 3RM.
- Ab work does not show up in volume-load calculations for the main lifts, but it adds systemic and trunk fatigue. Treating it as accessory volume to be cut (as the taper literature recommends) is a reasonable extrapolation, not a tested intervention.

### Gaps
- No taper study specifically manipulates accessory or core volume. All recommendations here extrapolate from total volume-load reductions.

## 6–7. Practical structure: flexion vs anti-movement days, circuits/supersets, time cost and session length

### Takeaway
Rotating between dynamic loaded flexion (hypertrophy-focused, e.g., cable crunch, hanging leg raise, weighted decline sit-up) and anti-extension/anti-rotation/anti-lateral-flexion work (bracing-focused, e.g., ab wheel, plank variants, Pallof, suitcase carry) covers both coach-recommended categories. Supersets cut session time about 50% with little volume loss, and pairing ab sets with rest periods of upper-body accessories adds almost no time.

### Cited Findings
- **[Coach consensus]** Nippard: best "total core" training uses weighted flexion plus anti-rotation plus anti-extension, progressed over time. — [FitnessVolt: Nippard 6-pack tips](https://fitnessvolt.com/jeff-nippard-6-pack-abs-tips/)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Core-stability exercises produced the greatest internal oblique activation, and traditional exercises (e.g., sit-ups) the greatest multifidus activation. Different exercise categories bias different core muscles, which supports rotating categories. — [Oliva-Lozano & Muyor 2020, IJERPH](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/12/4306)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld & Fimland (2021, *Sports Med*) concluded that supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve training time while keeping volume roughly intact. They recommend ≥4 weekly sets per muscle group in a 6–15RM range, and note that most studies used untrained or recreational participants. — [No Time to Lift? (PubMed 34125411)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/); [PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** In 29 resistance-trained participants, a traditional session produced about 4.2% more volume but lasted 23 minutes (66%) longer than an equivalent superset session. The superset session was rated harder for effort and discomfort. — [Andersen et al. 2022, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.912368/full)
- **[Peer-reviewed]** A 2025 meta-analysis (Zhang, Weakley et al.) found supersets shortened sessions without reducing volume load, but raised internal load (e.g., blood lactate). — [Superset vs traditional meta-analysis (PMC12011898)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/); [Leeds Beckett eprint](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908/1/SupersetVersusTraditionalResistanceTrainingPrescriptionsASystematicReviewAndMetaanalysisExploringAcuteAndChronicPV-WEAKLEY.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Supersets that cost almost no time:** pair ab sets with non-competing accessories already in the program. For example, Pallof press or dead bugs during rest between lateral raises or triceps work on push days, and cable crunches alternated with leg curls or calf raises on leg days. Do not superset abs with the main barbell lifts or heavy back-off sets, because trunk fatigue during those sets carries the stability concerns in section 3.
- **Finisher format:** a 6–10 minute end-of-session block of 2 exercises × 2–3 rounds (one flexion and one anti-movement) delivers about 4–6 sets per session. Over 4–5 sessions that reaches about 16–20 sets/week while adding only about 30–45 minutes/week in total.
- **Example accumulation-week template (inference, to be adjusted):**
  - Mon Legs A (after squats): cable crunch 3×10–15 + hanging leg raise 2×8–15 → 5 sets
  - Tue Push A: ab wheel or long-lever plank 2–3 sets + Pallof 2 sets/side, supersetted with accessories → 4–5 sets
  - Wed Pull A (after deadlifts): optional, or 2 light sets of side plank or suitcase carry → 0–2 sets
  - Thu Push B: weighted decline crunch 3×10–15 + dead bug 2 sets → 5 sets (moderate, since Fri is paused squat)
  - Fri Legs B (after paused squats): hanging leg raise or cable crunch 3 sets → 3 sets
  - Sat Pull B: suitcase carries or Pallof 2–3 sets (anti-lateral flexion; low fatigue before the Sunday rest) → 2–3 sets
  - Total ≈ 19–23 sets/week. Start near 12–14 in week 1 and build toward this by week 4–5, staying below RP's ~25-set MRV.
- Track trunk soreness and bracing quality on Monday and Wednesday as the autoregulation signal for ab volume.

### Gaps
- Superset studies used limb and compound exercises, not ab pairings, and mostly non-powerlifters. Time savings for ab-specific pairings are extrapolated.
- I found no study comparing flexion-focused vs anti-movement ab training for powerlifting performance outcomes.
