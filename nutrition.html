Nutrition + supplements

Nutrition

Prediction equations are a starting point, not an answer. In 285 athletes, measured resting metabolic rate came in below every equation, and Mifflin-St Jeor had the smallest error (Drexel study via Breaking Muscle). A 2023 meta-analysis found no equation guaranteed superior in athletes (Sports Med 2023). Mifflin gives this lifter roughly 2,000 kcal/day at rest. Standard activity multipliers (1.725–1.9, textbook values not validated here) put maintenance near 3,450–3,800 kcal. Iraki's off-season guidance of 42–48 kcal/kg/day, which already includes the surplus, works out to 3,800–4,350 kcal (Iraki 2019). Three thousand nine hundred kilocalories sits where both estimates overlap.

The rate of gain matters more than the starting number. Iraki recommends a 10–20% surplus for intermediates and 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week, which is 0.5–1.0 lb/week at 200 lb (Iraki 2019). Smaller surpluses look better the more trained the lifter is. Elite athletes eating ~600 kcal/day more gained no significantly more lean mass (+1.7 vs +1.2 kg) but significantly more fat (+1.1 vs +0.2 kg) (Garthe 2013 via Iraki). In Helms' 2023 RCT, a 15% surplus beat maintenance and a 5% surplus for bench 1RM but not for muscle thickness, and it added more skinfold fat (Helms 2023). The sample was small (17 completers). The plan therefore targets 0.5–0.75 lb/week (~6–9 lb over 12 weeks), the lower half of the range. The exact surplus needed to maximize muscle growth is unknown (Slater 2019). At 191 cm and 91 kg his normalized FFMI is probably about 20.5–21.5, depending on body fat. That is well below the ~25 that drug-free lifters rarely exceeded in Kouri's 1995 sample (BodySpec summary), so there is substantial room to grow. Because lean gain is slow in trained lifters, a sustained fast bulk would mostly add fat I.

Protein. Fat-free mass gains plateaued at ~1.6 g/kg/day in a 49-RCT meta-analysis (Morton 2018), and secondary commentaries put the upper confidence bound near 2.2 g/kg (Science for Sport). The plan targets 2.0–2.2 g/kg to cover that uncertainty. The old ~25 g per-meal ceiling is gone: 100 g of milk protein after training produced a larger and longer anabolic response than 25 g (Trommelen 2023). That was an acute study, so meal distribution is a secondary concern. Pre-sleep casein improved strength and size in a 12-week trial, though possibly only because it raised total protein (Snijders 2019). Carbohydrate and fat fill the rest of the budget. Iraki recommends at least 3–5 g/kg carbohydrate and 0.5–1.5 g/kg fat (Iraki 2019), and carbohydrate amount within reasonable ranges did not change lean gain in trained men (Ribeiro 2023 record).

Appetite is usually the obstacle for tall, active lifters. Athletes eating ad libitum tend to under-eat for their gaining goals; structured counseling raised intake by ~600 kcal/day in Garthe's study (via Iraki 2019), and Slater identifies satiety management as a core problem (Slater 2019). Practical fixes I are energy-dense foods (olive oil, nut butters, whole-fat dairy, granola, rice), a daily shake (whole milk, oats, peanut butter, banana, whey, ~800–1,000 kcal and ~50 g protein), fixed meal times, and tracking intake for at least the first 4–8 weeks. On interval days, add a carbohydrate-rich snack so the run does not erase the surplus.

Supplements

Creatine monohydrate is the clear first choice. The ISSN position stand reports 10–20% improvements in high-intensity repeated-effort performance after loading, with long-term safety in healthy people (ISSN 2017). The stand was funded by a supplement trade group. A 12-study meta-analysis found +1.14 kg lean mass from creatine plus training compared with training alone (Desai 2024). Some of that is water, which makes lean mass an imperfect proxy for muscle (Burke 2023). Caffeine at 3–6 mg/kg improves performance, with no added benefit at ≥9 mg/kg, though the evidence for maximal strength specifically is less certain (ISSN caffeine 2021). Vitamin D did not significantly improve strength in athletes overall, with possible small effects only when baseline levels were low (2023 meta-analysis). Beta-alanine helps 1–4-minute efforts more than strength (ISSN 2015), and omega-3 lacks hypertrophy evidence (Iraki 2019).
