# Load the abs hard, then cut to reveal them

The proposed schedule holds up well against the evidence. It puts **about 24 direct ab sets a week** at the end of sessions, spread across all six training days. It covers every ab function: loaded flexion, anti-extension, anti-rotation and anti-lateral flexion. It keeps only low-fatigue work on the day before heavy deadlifts, and it tapers ab work harder than the main lifts in weeks 6, 11 and 12. Five things need fixing. First, the jump from 6 to 24 sets in week 1 is bigger than the notes support: they recommend starting near 12–16 sets and building up, not only easing effort (RIR, reps in reserve) for two weeks. Second, 24 sets sits right at the coaching ceiling of about 25 a week, so it needs a clear rule for when to cut back. Third, a hard ab-roller session right after heavy deadlifts goes beyond the "light or optional" Wednesday the notes suggest. Fourth, adding load once you hit the top of the rep range only works for 4 of the 9 exercises. Fifth, the 72-hour no-hard-abs window before Monday's squat test reaches back into Friday and Saturday of week 11. The bigger caveat is that none of this will make abs visible on its own. Ab training does not burn local belly fat. Coaching estimates put a clear six-pack at roughly **10–12% body fat**, and home scales tend to read low. The evidence-backed sequence is to run this 12-week block at maintenance or a small surplus and finish the 1RM test, then cut at about **0.5–0.7% of bodyweight per week** with high protein, keeping the ab work going.

## Heavy lifts train the brace, not the six-pack muscles

The main reason to add direct ab work to a squat-, bench- and deadlift-heavy program is that the big lifts mostly train different trunk muscles than the ones people mean by "abs." A 2024 EMG study (which measures muscle electrical activity) compared back squats with dedicated trunk exercises. It found that squats work the **iliocostalis, multifidus and internal oblique** effectively, while dedicated exercises better suit the **external oblique and rectus abdominis** ([Fisioter. Mov.](https://www.scielo.br/j/fm/a/fzmbcFwdGtRGxJCWHbqcwGn/?lang=en)). Trunk activity during squats and deadlifts is "at least as high as" during stability-ball exercises, and ball work "may not provide enough stimulus for strength or hypertrophy" ([Nuzzo 2008](https://read.qxmd.com/read/18296961/trunk-muscle-activity-during-stability-ball-and-free-weight-exercises)). In other words, a 365 lb squatter already gets plenty of bracing work, but not much loaded work for the rectus abdominis or obliques. Coaches reach the same conclusion: compounds build trunk strength but probably not maximal rectus growth ([EliteFTS](https://elitefts.com/blogs/training/do-powerlifters-need-direct-abdominal-work)). No controlled trial has tested squats and deadlifts alone against squats and deadlifts plus direct ab work for ab growth, so this is an informed inference, not a settled result.

That points to **choosing exercises by function**: loaded spinal flexion for the rectus abdominis, plus anti-extension, anti-rotation and anti-lateral-flexion work for the obliques. Nippard's approach of weighted flexion plus anti-rotation plus anti-extension reflects the same idea ([FitnessVolt](https://fitnessvolt.com/jeff-nippard-6-pack-abs-tips/)). Two popular ideas don't hold up. The first is training "lower abs" separately. A 9-week trial in trained women found that "the manipulation of the exercise does not generate regionally different hypertrophy responses in the RA" ([UFMG thesis](https://repositorio.ufmg.br//bitstreams/ab000156-31a6-433f-8329-250395b2300a/download)). Hanging leg raises earn their place because they are hard and loadable, not because they target the lower abs. The second is ranking exercises purely by EMG. In one lab the ab wheel ranks among the best for the rectus ([Escamilla, JOSPT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16494072/)); in another it ranks well below a basic crunch ([Stenger thesis](https://minds.wisc.edu/items/13f23340-bc92-4049-9c47-6fcc60df9852/full)). Hanging knee-ups and rollout devices also produce **high hip-flexor activity**, which Escamilla's group flagged as a possible concern for people with low-back problems ([Escamilla, Phys Ther](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16649890/)). EMG is useful for ruling out weak options such as gadgets and easy planks. It cannot pick a winner among loaded crunch variations. Low-load stability work is a weak growth stimulus: in one trial rectus thickness increased only with general exercise, not core-stability training ([PubMed 27253335](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27253335/)).

Repeated spinal flexion is safe for a healthy lifter at these doses. The warnings against crunching come mainly from studies on pig spines bent roughly **4,400–86,400 times under compression**. Contreras and Schoenfeld concluded there is "simply not sufficient evidence" that healthy people should avoid crunches, though anyone with disc herniation or flexion intolerance should ([Contreras & Schoenfeld 2011](https://bretcontreras.com/wp-content/uploads/To-Crunch-or-Not-to-Crunch-An-Evidence-Based-Examination-of-Spinal-Flexion-Exercises-Their-Potential-Risks-and-Their-Applicability-to-Program-Design.pdf); [Schoenfeld summary](https://www.lookgreatnaked.com/blog/evidence-based-core-training/)). Trunk training also transfers to the barbell less than people assume. Meta-analyses find large gains in trunk strength itself but only **small-to-moderate effects on performance**, for example an effect size (SMD) of **0.39** for maximal strength ([Prieske 2016](https://www.pubmed.gov/26589515/); [Saeterbakken 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9213339/)). The powerlifting brace itself is built mostly under the bar. A belt raises intra-abdominal pressure by **25–40%** without lowering oblique EMG, so belts don't weaken the abs ([Lander 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1533266/)). Ab work here serves muscle size first and bracing endurance second; it should not be expected to lift the 1RMs.

## Twenty-four sets a week sits at the coaching ceiling

No peer-reviewed study sets volume targets specific to the abs, so the numbers come from coaching rules of thumb plus general dose-response research. Renaissance Periodization (RP) puts the ab minimum close to zero for lifters who train heavy compounds. It suggests about **6 sets a week as a starting point for experienced trainees**, says **16–20 sets** is a productive range, and warns that recovery problems tend to start **above about 25 sets a week**, trained 3–5 times a week ([RP Help Center](https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/32433381453463-Abs); [RP article](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/ab-training); [RP landmarks sheet](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1Dc0oZnkxcFVZ6Cl8whOs2O1AiXNKSkhRCRJmJ103_QA/edit)). Another secondary table lists a much lower ceiling of 12–20 sets, though it is unclear which muscle it refers to ([arvo.guru](https://arvo.guru/es/resources/methods/rp-training)). On the research side, the probability that more weekly sets bring more growth is effectively 100%, with diminishing returns. Once weekly volume is matched, training more often makes little difference for growth ([Pelland et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460)). Per-session returns flatten at about **11 fractional sets** for growth ([Remmert et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)), where an indirect set counts as half a set. That same method gives the abs partial credit for six barbell-heavy days a week, which is an argument against pushing direct work to the very top of the range.

For this lifter, that means **24 sets is defensible but sits at the ceiling**. It is four times his current 6 sets. It is above the 12–20 sets the programming notes call a reasonable target for heavy ab training, and it is well above what mainstream strength programs prescribe: 5/3/1 uses 50–100 reps in a single-leg/core slot, and PHAT variants use 2–3 sets of weighted crunches ([Lift Vault](https://liftvault.com/resources/main-supplemental-assistance-work/); [FitnessVolt PHAT](https://fitnessvolt.com/layne-nortons-phat-workout-for-size-and-strength/)). The split across sessions works well. The biggest day is 6 sets, far below the per-session flattening point, and the daily-session layout is about logistics, not physiology. The real limit is recovery for the heavy lifts. No study tests whether ab volume interferes with squat or deadlift performance, and nobody has measured how quickly the abs recover after hard sets, so the claim that "abs recover fast" is gym lore. That is why the plan needs a simple cut-back rule: **if bracing feels off or the low back is sore going into Monday or Wednesday, cut the loaded flexion and ab-roller sets before touching any main-lift work.**

## Ab work belongs after the barbell, and never hard the day before

The notes back placing all ab work at the end of sessions. No study has fatigued the abs specifically before barbell squats or deadlifts. But closely related research consistently shows harm. Core fatigue **decreased knee flexion and increased knee valgus** in squats ([Seyedi 2022](https://doaj.org/article/58d670ffd44048ab8b074bd06b54415d)). Lumbar-extensor fatigue changed lumbo-pelvic rhythm and loading at L5/S1 ([Hu & Ning, CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/203817/cdc_203817_DS1.pdf)). Trunk fatigue also reduced dynamic torso stability ([Gottipati dissertation](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstreams/0c12efa6-e605-4bfc-a2a1-5b9d006edc5e/download)). In contrast, deliberately bracing beforehand improved squat performance ([IJSPT via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5357260)). So warm-ups should include only brief bracing practice or McGill Big-3 style holds, done in low-fatigue reverse-pyramid sets ([ACE: McGill's Big Three](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/7077/low-back-exercises-stuart-mcgill-s-big-three/?topicScope=article)). Don't do ab circuits before squats or pulls.

The "day before" rule is a reasonable inference, not a tested finding: keep fatiguing flexion and long-lever anti-extension work off the day before heavy spinal loading. The notes suggest heavier loaded flexion on Monday and Friday, after squats. Tuesday and Thursday, the push days, get anti-movement work, with Thursday kept moderate because the paused squat follows on Friday. Saturday gets a light carry finisher, and Wednesday is optional and light. Ab sets can also be done during rest periods between non-competing accessories, such as lateral raises or leg curls. Supersets roughly **halve session time with minimal volume loss** ([Iversen et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/)). In trained lifters, traditional sessions ran **66% longer** for only about 4% more volume ([Andersen 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.912368/full)). Never pair ab work with the main barbell lifts or heavy back-off sets.

## Progress the crunches by load, everything else by leverage

There are no studies on ideal rep ranges, closeness to failure or tempo for the abs specifically, so progression follows general hypertrophy principles. The machine crunch, cable crunch and weighted decline crunch progress like any accessory: **8–15 reps (up to about 20) at 0–2 RIR**, adding 5–10 lb on the stack or a 2.5–5 kg plate once every set reaches the top of the range. Use a full range from slight spinal extension into full flexion, with the hips fixed so the movement is spinal flexion, not hip flexion. The claim that training the abs in the stretched position builds more muscle comes from limb studies ([lengthened partials, PMC11829627](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11829627/)) and has not been tested on the abs. Starting each crunch from slight extension costs nothing and may help. The other five exercises in the plan need their own progression ladders, set out in the recommendation table below. Low-load, high-frequency ab work, like the 2×10 bodyweight protocol done five days a week in Vispute's study, mostly builds endurance ([IDEA Fit summary](https://www.ideafit.com/crunches-donrsquot-cut-fat)). Long hold times are not a substitute for progressive load.

Ab work should be cut before the main lifts in deloads and tapers. Elite raw powerlifters cut training volume about **59%** in their taper and held their last session about **3.7 days** before competition ([Pritchard 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26670988/)). Pritchard's doctoral work recommends large volume cuts with intensity held roughly steady ([AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/10979)). The most common taper among 364 surveyed powerlifters was a 7–10 day step taper cutting volume-load by **41–50%** ([Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.735932/pdf)). The evidence on tapering for maximal strength is thin overall ([Travis et al. 2020](https://doaj.org/article/886093729f784abda2365f6ae95125f9)), and no taper study manipulates accessory or core volume specifically. So applying these cuts to ab work is a reasonable extrapolation, not a tested prescription.

## The recommended schedule, checked against the evidence

The weekly template for weeks 1–5 and 7–10 below is the schedule the coordinator is entering into the app. All ab work comes at the end of the session.

| Day | Main work | Ab work (end of session) | Sets | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Legs A, heavy squat | Hanging leg raise 3×10–15; ab crunch machine 3×10–15 (RIR 0–2) | 6 | Loaded flexion |
| Tue | Push A, heavy bench (day before deadlift) | Pallof press 2×10/side; side bridge 2×30 s/side | 4 | Anti-rotation, anti-lateral flexion |
| Wed | Pull A, heavy deadlift | Ab roller 3×8–12 (RIR 1–2) | 3 | Anti-extension |
| Thu | Push B, OHP + bench volume | Weighted decline crunch 3×10–15; standing cable wood chop 2×10–12/side | 5 | Loaded flexion, rotation |
| Fri | Legs B, paused squat | Cable crunch 3×10–15 | 3 | Loaded flexion |
| Sat | Pull B, RDL + back | One-hand farmer's walk (suitcase carry) 3×30–40 m/side | 3 | Anti-lateral flexion |
| **Total** | | | **24** | 12 flexion, 3 anti-extension, 2 rotation, 7 low-fatigue anti-movement |

Weeks 1–2 ramp in at RIR 2–3. Progression is double progression throughout. Week 6 drops to about half the sets at RIR 3–4. Week 11 cuts about 60%, mostly anti-movement and easy. In week 12 there is no hard ab work within about 72 hours before the Monday squat test or the Thursday deadlift test. That week has a light Pallof press on Tuesday, cable crunches after the deadlift test on Thursday, and leg raises plus side bridge on Friday.

**What the evidence supports.** Placing everything at the end of sessions matches every relevant study. The plan covers every ab function the notes recommend, and the four loaded flexion movements are the most loadable options with the highest EMG. Putting the hardest flexion on Monday and Friday, after squats, matches the notes' layout. Thursday's decline crunch plus a second exercise for 5 sets matches the notes' sample Thursday almost exactly; the notes used dead bugs as the second exercise. The Tuesday low-fatigue rule before deadlifts and the Saturday carry after RDLs follow the notes' "day before" logic. Halving volume in week 6, cutting about 60% in week 11 (the notes say 50–70%), and the 72-hour rule in week 12 all fall within the ranges the notes recommend.

## Visible abs depend on body fat, and the cut comes after the test

No peer-reviewed study sets a body-fat threshold for visible abs. Coaching sources put first upper-ab outlines at about **14–15%** and a clear six-pack at **10–12%** ([InBody](https://inbodyusa.com/blogs/inbodyblog/what-body-fat-percentage-do-you-need-to-see-abs/)). Henselmans' review of 50 DEXA scans found that at about 10%, most men "if you're muscular enough… have a full six-pack" ([Henselmans](https://mennohenselmans.com/what-body-fat-percentages-really-look-like-50-actual-mens-dexa-scans/)). That is where the ab hypertrophy work pays off: thicker muscle probably shows through slightly more fat, though nobody has measured how much. Measurement error is as large as the targets themselves. Bioimpedance scales like the InBody "consistently underestimated percent body fat" compared with DXA ([Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1240252/full)). Across field methods, biases ran from **−1.8 to −10.7 percentage points** ([PMC10453806](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10453806)). A home-scale "11%" may be 13–15% on DEXA. Waist circumference at the navel, standardized photos and the trend in morning bodyweight are better guides than any single device reading.

The 24 weekly sets will not burn belly fat. In Vispute's trial, six weeks of ab training improved ab endurance but did not change body fat, waist circumference or abdominal fat ([NSCA summary](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/infographics/trainer-tips-is-spot-reduction-a-thing/)). A 2022 meta-analysis of **13 studies and 1,158 people** found "no spot reduction, regardless of the characteristics of the population and of the exercise program" ([Ramírez-Campillo 2022](https://doi.org/10.5114/HM.2022.110373)). A single 2023 trial claims local fat loss from ab endurance work plus running ([Physiological Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10680576)). It stands against a larger body of null results, and any effect would be far too small to replace a calorie deficit.

The nutrition evidence fits together well. Aim to lose **0.5–1% of bodyweight per week** ([Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)), slowing to **0.5% or less** as you get leaner ([Roberts 2020](https://johk.pl/?p=5386)). In Garthe's study, elite athletes losing about **0.7%/week gained lean mass (about 2%) and 1RM strength**, while those losing about 1.4%/week did not significantly gain lean mass ([Garthe 2011](https://www.pubmed.gov/21558571/)). A meta-analysis found that deficits around **500 kcal/day stopped lean-mass gains**, though strength gains were not significantly worse; most subjects in those trials were untrained ([Murphy & Koehler 2022](https://www.pubmed.gov/34623696/)). Protein needs rise during a cut. The benefit for muscle gain plateaus around **1.6 g/kg** at maintenance ([Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)), but lean lifters in a deficit should aim for **1.8–2.7 g/kg** (about 0.8–1.2 g/lb), spread across 4–5 meals ([Roberts 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7052702)). Sleep and alcohol matter too. Sleeping 5.5 hours instead of 8.5 during a diet cut the share of weight lost as fat by about **55%** ([Nedeltcheva 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921542/)). Post-workout alcohol cut muscle protein synthesis by **24–37%** ([Parr 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24533082/)). Avoid water or sodium cuts for appearance ([Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)).

That leads to a clear sequence. Run the 12-week block, especially weeks 11–12, at **maintenance or a small surplus**, because peaking and a true 1RM test need stable bodyweight and full readiness. No trial has compared peaking in a deficit with peaking at maintenance, but the deficit studies give no reason to risk it. If the lifter is currently around 15–20% body fat, a mild recomposition deficit of **0.5%/week or less** during weeks 1–10 is viable. Trained lifters can recompose with progressive overload, high protein and a modest deficit ([Barakat 2020](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4066252?lng=en)). Then return to maintenance for the peak. After week 12, take a 1–2 week transition, then run a dedicated cut at 0.5–0.7%/week. At a 90 kg (about 200 lb) example bodyweight, going from about 16% to 11% means losing about 5 kg of fat, which takes **roughly 10–12 weeks**. During the cut, keep heavy top sets, trim accessory volume only if recovery suffers, and keep the direct ab work. The ab muscle built during this block is what lets the abs show at the higher end of that 10–12% range.

## Conclusion

The schedule's structure is sound. The real risks are timing and how fast it ramps up, not the exercises chosen. The two changes that matter most are spreading the 6-to-24-set increase over three or four weeks and moving the week 11 Friday and Saturday work out of the 72-hour window before the squat test. Both come from the same principle the plan already uses: ab fatigue should never land in front of the heaviest spinal loading. The less obvious point is that the ab block and the cut work together. Twelve weeks of loaded flexion builds the muscle thickness that lets abs show at a higher body fat, but only a deficit run after the test removes the fat covering them. Every ab-specific number here, from 25 sets as a ceiling to the 10–12% visibility estimate, rests on coaching rules of thumb and limb-muscle research rather than ab trials. The lifter's own Monday and Wednesday bracing quality and his waist and photo trend are better feedback than any rule in this report.
